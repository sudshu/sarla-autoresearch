Two hills, one forest: when the best-fitting answer is not the most likely one

A walk through one week of results from the SARLA / CARDAMOM project at the NL-Loobos flux tower, written for someone starting graduate work in Earth-system science or statistics. Companion to the autoresearch progress page; code and numbers in the repository. Hover any chart for values; every chart has a table view.

The question, and why anyone should care

How long does carbon stay locked up in a tree's wood before it goes back to the atmosphere? Ten years and forty years are both "decades", but for a climate projection they are very different forests: one returns its carbon quickly, the other is a slow, long-lived store. This page is about a single Scots pine stand in the Netherlands, planted in 1911 and never logged, where a widely used carbon-cycle model cannot make up its mind between those two answers, and about what happens when you ask "which is right?" in the two ways scientists usually ask it.

Along the way it turns into a small lesson in Bayesian inference that textbooks state but rarely show with real data: the most probable single answer and the most probable kind of answer can be different things, and a fast method that finds one may tell you nothing about the other.

The setup in three paragraphs

The model. DALEC is a box model of a forest's carbon: leaves, wood, roots, litter and soil, with carbon flowing between them at rates set by 89 parameters (photosynthesis capacity, how growth is split between leaves and wood, how fast wood dies, how fast litter decomposes, and so on). CARDAMOM is the framework that fits those parameters to data at a site, here 16 years of monthly photosynthesis, net carbon exchange, evapotranspiration and leaf area from the tower, plus one biomass measurement. It also imposes "ecological and dynamical constraints" (EDCs): rules such as roots must not turn over slower than wood or carbon pools must not drift far from equilibrium over the record. Some EDCs are hard walls (a parameter set that violates them is simply forbidden), others are soft penalties.

The landscape. Think of the 89 parameters as coordinates and the log-posterior as altitude: how well a parameter set fits the data, plus how well it respects the constraints, on a log scale. A difference of 10 units in altitude means one point is e10 ≈ 22,000 times more probable than the other, so the landscape is extremely steep. The MAP ("maximum a posteriori") is the highest summit. The posterior mass of a region is how much probability it holds, roughly its area times its height; a wide, lower hill can hold far more mass than a narrow, taller one.

Two ways to explore it. A sampler (MCMC) is a hiker who wanders the landscape spending time in each place in proportion to its probability; after long enough, where the hiker has been is the posterior. CARDAMOM's sampler, ADEMCMC, takes about 33 hours per site. An optimiser is a climber who only ever goes uphill; it finds a summit fast but learns nothing about how much land lies around it. SARLA is a fast (~30-minute) GPU method that tries to get the sampler's answer at the optimiser's price, by finding the summits first and then wandering around each.

Try it: a tall narrow hill and a wide low one

Before the real data, a toy. Two hills in one dimension. Drag the sliders and watch the two summaries: the peak (which hill has the highest point) and the mass (which hill holds more probability). They agree only when the hills have similar widths.

Take-away: "most probable point" and "most probable region" are different questions. On this toy you can make hill B hold 90% of the mass while hill A keeps the summit. Now watch the same thing happen with 89 dimensions of real forest.

The two stories the data allow

Story A: fast turnoverAbout 80% of the trees' new growth goes into wood, and that wood is lost again after ~10 years on average. A high-throughput forest. Ecologically odd for a 100-year-old unmanaged pine stand, but it reproduces the observations.
Story B: slow turnoverAbout 20% of growth goes into wood, and it lasts ~40 years. Close to what forest inventories and litterfall measurements at this site suggest. It also reproduces the observations.

Both stories match the single biomass measurement the model is given, and both fit the carbon fluxes to within measurement noise. The data alone barely distinguish them. So: which is more probable?

Figure 1. Where is the probability, and where is the peak?

We ran the 33-hour sampler with 64 independent chains and checked that they agree (a standard convergence test, R̂ < 1.1 on all 89 parameters). The plot summarises its draws along one axis, the fraction of new growth that goes into wood, which is what separates the two stories. Toggle between the two summaries.

Table view

Mass view: 82% of the sampler's draws are on the fast-turnover story. Story B is real but a minority. Peak view: the highest point anywhere (log-posterior −161.9, found by an optimiser) is on the slow-turnover story, 30 units above the best point the sampler itself ever visited. The tall hill is the narrow one; the wide hill is the lower one.

Take-away: this is not a bug in either method. It is what a bimodal posterior looks like when one mode is sharp and the other broad. Notice the orange bars: the fast method gets the weights backwards, putting most of its draws on the slow-turnover hill, because its walkers rarely cross from one hill to the other. Fixing that is the current job of the autoresearch loop.

Figure 2. If we knew the truth, which summary would get it right?

With real data nobody knows the true parameters. So we manufactured a case where we do: pick a parameter set as "truth", run the model to make fake observations with realistic noise, and fit the model to those. This is called an OSSE (observing system simulation experiment). The truth was drawn from the sampler's dominant story, A. We then asked an optimiser to find the best fit to the fake data.

Table view

The best fit lands on story B, 61 log-units "better" than the truth, which lives on story A. Remember the scale: 61 log-units is e61, an astronomically higher density. And it is still the wrong story. A product that reports the best fit would report the wrong wood lifetime with great confidence. The sampler's mass (~80% on story A) would get it right.

Take-away: the highest point of a posterior is not a safe summary when the posterior has more than one hill. This is the one result on the page that a reviewer cannot argue with, because the truth is known.

Figure 3. How fast can a climber reach the top, and does it matter?

Finding a summit is an optimisation problem. Textbooks say Newton's method, which uses the local curvature of the landscape (the 89×89 matrix of second derivatives, the Hessian), climbs fastest. We raced three climbers from the same 31 starting points: damped Newton with the exact Hessian, Gauss-Newton (a cheaper approximation to the Hessian that is standard in weather data assimilation), and L-BFGS (which builds up a rough picture of curvature from the steps it has already taken). All were forced to stay inside the hard constraints.

Table view

Both curvature-based climbers stall after ~20 steps, 25 log-units below the summit, and never recover. The humble L-BFGS reaches the top in about 30 minutes. Why? The hills here are not domes; they are long, nearly flat ridges with hard walls along their sides. A quadratic model of a ridge sends its step off the ridge or into a wall, the step is rejected, and the method shrinks to tiny gradient steps. L-BFGS learns the ridge direction from where it has walked. And notice the grey dot: after 31.6 hours the sampler's best draw is 30 units below L-BFGS's 30-minute result. Samplers are not built to find summits, and optimisers are not built to weigh hills.

Take-away: the right tool depends on the question. If you want the best fit, use L-BFGS from several good starts. If you want to know which story is more probable, no optimiser will tell you.

Figure 4. What actually separates the two hills?

If the hills were separated by a deep valley of poor data fit, the two stories would be genuinely distinct hypotheses. If they are separated by the model's own constraints, that is a different matter. We walked from one hill to the other and split the altitude into its two parts: the data term (how well observations are matched) and the constraint term (the soft ecological penalties). Two routes: a straight line in parameter space, and the routes the sampler actually took when it crossed.

Table view

The straight line drops ~700 log-units, almost all of it constraint penalty, because a straight line between two sensible forests passes through nonsensical ones (carbon pools exploding or collapsing). The sampler's own crossings, by contrast, dip only 10 to 40 log-units, and the sampler crosses the midpoint 30 to 50 times in a few thousand steps. For the slow hiker, the two stories are not separate mountains but two bumps on one connected ridge with a constraint-textured surface. The fast method's walkers, each confined to a local patch, never make that crossing. That is exactly why their hill weights come out wrong.

Take-away: "multimodal" is a statement about the geometry and about how you move through it. The same posterior is one ridge to a sampler with long-range moves and two islands to a sampler without them.

Could two Hessians replace the sampler for the hill weights?

A tempting shortcut. If each hill were a smooth dome, its volume could be read off the curvature at its summit (a Laplace approximation), and the ratio of volumes would give the weights in minutes. We tried it with the two summits from Figure 2.

It fails, and the diagnostics say why: at both "summits" the curvature has 9 to 13 negative directions (they are points pressed against constraint walls, not interior maxima), 13 to 21 directions flatter than the prior, and only 1% of each fitted Gaussian lies inside the region the constraints allow. The Gaussian describes a region that mostly does not exist. On real data one convention happens to land near the sampler's 0.82 and another at 0.49; on the synthetic data every convention gives 0.000 for the hill that holds the truth. Any weight estimator built on one Gaussian per hill (Laplace, bridge sampling with Gaussian proposals, Gaussian importance sampling) fails here for the same reason.

The wood budget behind each story

What the two stories mean physically. We pushed 500 parameter sets from each method through the model and averaged over the 16 years. The last column is what the site's own measurements suggest (forest inventory, needle litterfall, European pine mortality rates), none of which were given to the model.

Both fit the one biomass observation. The sampler's dominant story does so with a wood pool that turns over every 10 years and puts four times less into needles than the measured litterfall. Whether that is telling us something about the site or about the model's constraints is an open question, and a good one.

Where the fix is being searched

An automated research loop is testing variants of the fast method. Each is scored by a calibration gap G (0 perfect, ~0.3 is noise between repeated runs, 5 means the wrong hill entirely) and, since this analysis, by whether its hill weights are stable across random seeds. Nothing has passed both tests yet.

The pattern is consistent with Figure 4. Better local moves and a better map of the summits improve the score but leave the hill weights at the mercy of where walkers start. "Tempering" (letting hot copies of the walker roam a flattened landscape and swapping with the cold one) does not help when only the data term is flattened, and does help when the constraint terms are flattened too. That is the pass in Figure 4 showing up in the sampler's behaviour. A version that flattens only the three constraint terms that carry the pass is queued.

Caveats, honestly

  • One site, one vegetation type (evergreen needleleaf), one climate. Nothing here is shown to generalise.
  • The synthetic truth was drawn from story A by construction. Had it been drawn from story B, the best fit would have been right. The lesson is that peak and mass can disagree, not that either story is the truth.
  • Both summits sit against a fire-related constraint that the data at this site (no fires) cannot see. It affects no quantity shown.
  • The hard constraint has a floating-point knife-edge: the same 16 starting points were all feasible on the GPU and 11 of 16 on the CPU. Values here come from one backend.

Open problems a student could take on

  • Estimate hill weights without Gaussians. Something between "sample for 33 hours" and "fit a Gaussian per hill": nested sampling, path sampling along the real ridge, or a mixture built from the sampler's own crossings.
  • Is the equilibrium constraint right for a growing forest? This stand gained ~40% biomass over 25 years. The constraint penalises drift from equilibrium. Quantify what it costs the slow-turnover story, and test alternatives at sites with repeated inventories.
  • Break the tie with data. Needle litterfall and biomass increments are measured here but were not assimilated. Which observation, at what precision, collapses the posterior to one story? That is an observing-system design question.
  • Do the two stories predict differently? Under a warming or drought scenario, do stories A and B diverge in net carbon exchange? If they do, the choice of summary is a climate-relevant decision, not a statistical nicety.
  • Report the right thing. Products built on the best fit would report a 40-year wood lifetime at this site; products built on the posterior median would report 10 years. Which do current global carbon products do, and does it matter at scale?
Glossary
posteriorThe probability of each parameter set given the data, the prior and the constraints. The object every method here is trying to describe.
log-posteriorIts logarithm; the "altitude" in the landscape picture. A gap of 10 means e10 ≈ 22,000× more probable.
MAPMaximum a posteriori: the single highest point. The "best fit".
posterior massHow much probability a region holds. Height times extent. A wide low hill can outweigh a tall narrow one.
mode / hillA local maximum and the region around it. A posterior with two of them is bimodal.
MCMC / samplerAn algorithm that wanders the landscape so that time spent in each place matches its probability. ADEMCMC is CARDAMOM's; it takes ~33 h here.
optimiserAn algorithm that only climbs. L-BFGS, Newton and Gauss-Newton are optimisers.
HessianThe matrix of second derivatives: local curvature. Used by Newton's method and by the Laplace approximation.
Laplace approximationReplacing a hill by a Gaussian with the same summit and curvature, to estimate its volume cheaply.
EDCEcological and dynamical constraint: a rule the parameters must obey. Hard EDCs forbid; soft EDCs penalise.
OSSEA synthetic experiment with a known truth, used to test whether a method recovers what it should.
temperingRunning extra copies of a sampler on flattened versions of the landscape so they can cross barriers, and swapping states with the real one.
calibration gap GThe loop's score: how far a method's uncertainty ranges are from containing the truth as often as they claim.
